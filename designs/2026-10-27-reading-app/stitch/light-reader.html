The Long Noon

Section 2 · Orbital Decay Sequence

The thermal horizon refused to subside. Above the titanium truss of Station Echo-9, the twin suns hung pinned at an unyielding zenith, casting shadows that were no longer silhouettes but razor-thin gouges along the dust-white deck plates. For thirty planetary cycles, day had forgotten how to end.

Kade adjusted her ocular shade, hearing the servo hum through the bone of her temple. In the lower atmospheric bay, the coolant reservoirs were wheezing under atmospheric turbulence. She touched the cold bronze regulator, wondering how many liters remained before the solar flares stripped their primary shielding down to pure carbon.

"Memory is simply the lingering light of stars that have already burned through their own fuel," she murmured into the log console, unwilling to turn around.

A synthetic raven wheeled through the exhaust vents high above, its carbon-fiber wings ticking against the heat grids. In the academy textbooks back on Ceres, they called this phenomenon the Solstice Stasis—a harmonic alignment occurring once every four decades. But textbooks never accounted for the dry metallic taste of recycled oxygen or the quiet panic of three engineers realizing the beacon array had desynchronized.

She tapped the terminal membrane twice. The frequency waveform flickered in faint amber, stuttering against interference from the outer corona. Every calculation pointed toward the same conclusion: if the orbital tether didn’t release before perihelion, the station would not drift into darkness; it would simply dissolve into the glare.
